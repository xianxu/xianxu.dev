In this post, hot take on how we leverage AI. 

## What is AI good at?

#### 1.

AI (LLM) has all the world's knowledge. It's trained with basically all the written and increasingly probably all the digital assets we have ever accumulated as a species. So often, for each specific domain, especially in domains operator is not an expert, AI will be more knowledgeable than you. 

#### 2.

General purpose LLM is good at follow some small-scale logical consistency. What I meant small-scale: short reasoning chains before grounding. Errors compound with every step taken without grounding: if each step is right with probability $p$, a sequence of $n$ steps is right with $p^n$. Better models raise $p$, but don't change the exponent. Not even a human can write perfect code without a compiler. So I don't think one-shotting a very deep sequence of actions is something that will be solved; grounding is what resets the chain.  

#### 3.

It seems not yet have good appreciation of relative importance of different competing facts. You may say they lack the good intuition and "common sense" human expert has, for example, regarding system architecture, user ergonomics, performance etc. I used the word "general purpose LLM" here, as I don't think it's the case that such intuition is human only, as for example, AlphaGo/AlphaZero seems to have pretty good intuition in playing the game Go. It seems those intuitions are deep domain understanding, sort of needing customized training. General purpose LLM still lacks such specialization. This on the other hand, seems to indicate if the economical value is great, then likely such more specialized "LLM" will emerge. Or they will emerge when "LLM" learning efficiency increase by orders of magnitudes.

#### 4.

Combining [1.](#1) and [2.](#2), they are pretty good at following rules and process, at reasonable length of steps without definitive grounding steps. 

## What does this mean for AI coding? 

#### 5.

Decompose system change into shorter steps. Improve performance by **a)** either providing definitive grounding after those short steps; or **b)** at least provide some guidance on the shape of some of the intermediate state. In AI coding, this means compile often, so language with fast compile/test cycle will have advantage. And having some process, like asking to write plan know, asking to write domain entity down. 

#### 6.

From point [4.](#4), make the software development, product development process legible to AI and let it drive. AI native company is real, but errors compound. We need to build one step at a time. 

#### 7.

I do not believe that human can or should review AI generated code. First of all, AI generate code fast, and is a function of how much token you use. Human will never keep up with that speed. Human, in order to review code, needs to have a good mental model of the code base itself. While during the initial introduction of AI coding to a system, human can still review as they have accumulated enough knowledge, this advantage will quickly be erode as more code is generated by AI. If we insist human to review code, then the review will become the bottleneck. Plus, in special domains, e.g. security, AI reviewer likely will outperform human finding arcane issues. Review does keep a human's mental model of the system alive, and that matters, but reading code is a very expensive way to get it. A cheaper way is for the human to own the system's invariants (the state model, the data model, the interface contracts) and let the machine own the code. [10.](#10), [11.](#11) and [12.](#12) are how I check them. 

#### 8.

I do not believe in spec-driven development, popularized by tools like [Spec Kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit), [Kiro](https://kiro.dev) and [OpenSpec](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec). All those spec-driven development uses human language to describe system behavior. Human language is notoriously imprecise and I can't help but feeling using such an imprecise language to describe all the details of system behavior missed the whole point: we may well write code ourselves. The precise kind of spec is code: types, schemas, property tests. That's where the invariants from [7.](#7) belong, so the machine can check them. 

#### 9.

I don't review machine generated specs in detail neither, for similar reasons to [8.](#8) Those specs, and engineering plans tend to be bloated. I view those plans as form the corpus that influence how machine generate, i.e. point [5.b)](#5). 

#### 10.

I identify issues in several different ways. The first is to talk about expected surface behavior, based on my understanding of how I want the system to behave. It is a lot easier for human to understand and reason about those surface behaviors, than the detailed construction of underlying components. This can be viewed as a form of [5.b)](#5) as well. 

#### 11.

The second is to review state model I ask AI to generate as part of their engineering plan. It's a small table in that big file. More often than not, I will notice that portion is both over engineered: too many state that I find reasoning about them hard; and also under engineered, partly due to the over engineering of state to begin with, that machine often leave system in limbo state, that end user can't recover from. It's a form of on paper that state exists, but then also ultimately useless. I view this as the [5.b)](#5) check as well, and likely such piece of data that worth reviewing, should be extracted at and presented to human at a better place.

#### 12.

The third is to smoke test. That's the ultimate grounding step human is still currently the gate keeper. Eat your dog food become a lot more important. I frequently identify issues, both ergonomic, but also performance issues, and uncover quite immature design choices. 

#### 13.

I believe AI can help improve those manual checks I do in [10.](#10), [11.](#11), [12.](#12) I think those improvements will come from harnesses and human/machine interface design. I also believe [3.](#3) (specialized architecture design model etc.) will arrive eventually, those are going to be the core model improvements. 

#### 14.

I believe we don't leverage AI enough in [1.](#1) (world knowledge) when it comes to AI coding. If machine can code, they should be able to design sensible end user flow directly with minimal human input. 

#### 15.

I don't use MCP. MCP continues the walled garden of fragmented context. Your data stays in each vendor's system, and AI only sees the slice that vendor's MCP server exposes, one call at a time, rather than having everything locally where it can search across all of it. And you get to pay the toll every time. The higher cost may be justified, since agents might access the data inefficiently and a lot more often than traditional human-generated traffic, but the per-call access itself is an architectural limit of MCP. If you must use a vendor system, consider fully synchronizing its data into your own repository, where AI can search it like everything else. Given how easy it is to code custom internal workflows with AI coding, I believe organizations should invest in dedicated resources to start in-sourcing more traditional SaaS, making them flexibly accessible to internal AI systems. With SaaS, you also pay for 99 features you don't use, and for the vendor's on-call behind them. When you are small, you don't care about compliance; you need a business first. When you are big enough, you can hire a proper SRE team to run it, again with the help of AI.
